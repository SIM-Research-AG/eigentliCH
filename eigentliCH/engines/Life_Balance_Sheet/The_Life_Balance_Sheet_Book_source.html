Preface

Why a balance sheet for a life

This book takes an idea from institutional finance — asset–liability management — and asks what happens if we apply it honestly to a single human life.

A pension fund does not measure success by how much cash it holds. It asks whether its assets will meet its liabilities as they fall due, under uncertainty, with an acceptable margin of safety. That question — not “how much do we have?” but “will what we have meet what we owe, on time, probably?” — turns out to be exactly the right one to ask of a person deciding how to spend the next decade.

The trouble with money-only planning is not that money is unimportant; it is that money is downstream. What actually produces the money, and the life, is a set of slower-moving capitals: what you know, who you know, and the health and hours you have to deploy them. Those capitals grow in a characteristic way, they feed one another, and they decay if neglected. A model that keeps them on the balance sheet can say things a cash-flow spreadsheet cannot — most usefully, what an hour of your time is actually worth, and toward which goal.

The book has four parts. Part I motivates the reframe and the dynamical-systems view. Part II builds the model in full: the state, the controls, the stochastic dynamics, the goals, the notion of feasibility, and the optimisation that turns all of it into a recommendation. This part is deliberately mathematical; readers who want the argument without the machinery can follow the prose and skip the displayed equations without losing the thread. Part III works five lives in detail, each chosen to stress a different part of the model, and each an instance of the one framework rather than a model of its own. Part IV is candid about what the model cannot do, and where it should go next. Everything numerical here is illustrative, chosen to make the behaviour legible; none of it is financial advice.

A note on this edition. The model now carries a standard-of-living habit as a state variable, names the first portfolio role Gain, and treats the macro regime as a layer the adviser can switch on to couple a personal plan to the wider capital cycle. These are set out where they belong, in Chapters 3 to 6 and 11, and they are the through-line to the author's institutional work.

Chapter 1

The reframe

Three moves turn a life into something an asset–liability manager would recognise. Each is small on its own; together they change what questions we can ask.

Move one: capitals are assets

We treat everything that carries value across time as an asset. Financial wealth is the obvious one, but it is joined by expertise (what you can do), network (who will act on your behalf), and health (the energy that makes the other three usable). These are not metaphors. Each can be invested in, each yields a return, and each depreciates if left alone. The difference from money is only that we lack a tidy account statement for them — which is precisely the gap the model fills.

Move two: goals are liabilities

A milestone — a home, a company, financial independence, retirement — is a claim: an amount, due at a date, that the assets must be able to meet. Framing goals this way imports the whole apparatus of liability management: matching, funding ratios, duration, and the discipline of asking not whether a goal is nice to have but whether it is funded.

Definition 1.1 — Liability

A goal is a triple (deadline, region, confidence): a time by which it must be met, a set of states that count as meeting it, and the probability of success the planner requires. A goal with no required confidence is a wish, not a liability.

Move three: the saving rate is an output, and time is the constraint

The two moves above are natural; the third is where most planning goes wrong. People treat the saving rate as a dial they set. It is not. The saving rate is what remains after consumption and self-investment — an output of the plan, not an input to it. Set the inputs and the saving rate is determined; try to set the saving rate directly and you have merely hidden the real decisions.

And the truly scarce resource is not money but time. Money can be borrowed, saved, and compounded; the twenty-four-hour day cannot. Every hour spent earning is an hour not spent learning, connecting, or recovering. A plan that does not treat the time budget as a hard constraint will always look more feasible on paper than it is in a life — because on paper, time is free.

Assumption 1.2 — Time is binding

The allocation of time is constrained to a fixed budget each period and cannot be stored or transferred across periods. This single assumption is responsible for most of the model’s more useful and less obvious recommendations.

The reward for the extra structure is a richer question. A money-only tool asks “will I have enough?” This model asks “what should I do this period, given everything I am trying to become, and what is the marginal hour worth toward each goal?” The rest of the book is the machinery that answers it.

Chapter 2

A person as a dynamical system

If capitals are assets and goals are liabilities, then a life is a controlled dynamical system: a state that evolves over time, steered by decisions, disturbed by chance, aiming to reach target regions.

Control theory gives us the vocabulary. A state summarises everything about the present that matters for the future. A control is what the decision-maker can set. Dynamics describe how the state responds to the control and to random disturbance. A goal is a region of the state space we would like to enter; feasibility is the question of whether the reachable set of the controlled system intersects that region before the deadline. This is the same structure an engineer uses to regulate a plant — applied, here, to a career and a portfolio.

The characteristic motion of each capital is the S-curve. Growth is slow while a capital is small, accelerates once it is large enough to reinforce itself (skill compounds; networks introduce you to more networks), and flattens as it approaches a natural ceiling. This is the logistic law, and it is why timing matters so much: the same effort produces almost nothing in the slow phase and a great deal in the accelerating one.

Goals-as-regions is not a formality. Because the capitals are distinct dimensions, a goal can require several of them at once — and no amount of one can substitute for a shortfall in another. Founding a company is the clean example: money without a network, or a network without the competence to use it, does not clear the bar. The phase portrait below shows why the path you take through the state space, not just the effort you expend, decides whether you arrive.

With the picture in place, Part II makes each element precise: what exactly is in the state, what the controls are and how they are constrained, the equations of motion, the shape of the goals, and the optimisation that chooses the controls.

Chapter 3

The state: four capitals and a habit

The state is what we must carry forward in time. Four capitals suffice, with wealth split into three components because a single number cannot tell an honest financial story, and alongside them a standard-of-living habit that turns out to have consequences.

x=(WL,WR,D,E,N,H,κ∣m)(3.1)

Here WL is liquid financial wealth (cash and marketable investments), WR is real or illiquid assets (property, private stakes), and D is debt. Splitting wealth this way is not fastidiousness. A person can be wealthy in a house they live in and unable to spend a franc of it; a leveraged asset behaves nothing like cash. The distinction becomes decisive for the independence goal in Chapter 7 and for the worked lives of Part III. Net worth is the derived quantity

W=WL+WR−D(3.2)

The next three capitals are dimensionless indices, normalised so that a well-developed professional sits near 1.0: E for expertise, N for network, and H for health, bounded in [0,1] and acting as an efficiency multiplier on everything the person does. Health earns its place in the state precisely because it closes a feedback loop a static plan would miss: overwork erodes it, and its erosion quietly lowers the return on all the other capitals.

Carried alongside the capitals is a standard-of-living habit κ, a stock that tracks recent consumption. It is on by default, because it is not a bare reference point but a stock with consequences: a maintained lifestyle keeps a person in the rooms and memberships that raise the network (and, weakly, the expertise) ceiling, so cutting spending costs more than the spending itself. The habit is a scalar carried beside the four capitals, and Chapter 5 gives its law of motion.

The final entry, the macro regime m, is optional and written after the bar in (3.1). Switched off, the return parameters are constant and the reader may ignore it; switched on, it couples the personal problem to the capital cycle, and Chapter 11 says how. It takes values across the phases from boom to crisis.

Definition 3.1 — Capital

A capital is a stock that (i) carries value across periods, (ii) can be increased by allocating time or money, (iii) yields a flow return, and (iv) depreciates without maintenance. Wealth satisfies (i)–(iii) and, uniquely, does not depreciate — it earns a return instead. The habit κ is not a capital in this sense: it is a reference stock that shapes utility and lifts ceilings, not an asset one draws on.

Two properties distinguish the capitals mathematically, and Chapter 5 respects both. Expertise, network and health saturate — each rises toward a ceiling. Wealth accumulates — it compounds without one. Modelling all four with a single tidy law would be elegant and wrong.

Chapter 4

The controls: two budgets and a portfolio

You cannot move the capitals directly. You move them by allocating time and money each period, and by choosing a portfolio. The control vector collects every lever the planner actually holds.

u=(τ,C,mE,mN,pA,w∣jumps)(4.1)

The time shares τ = (τY, τE, τN, τH) divide the productive day between earning, learning, networking and recovery. The money flows are consumption C, education and network spend mE, mN, and amortisation pA. The portfolio w is discussed just below. Note what is not a control: the saving rate. It falls out of the money flows, exactly as argued in Chapter 1.

The portfolio and its four roles

The portfolio is expressed not as a list of asset classes but as weights on four roles, the same taxonomy the author’s institutional Optimizer uses, so that a personal plan and a pension fund share one calibration.

Gain growth assets that compound the estate: broad equity and equity-like risk.
Income cash-flow assets: yielding credit, dividend and rental streams.
Stabilization diversifiers that cushion drawdowns: high-grade duration and the like.
Protection convex tail hedges and cash that pay off precisely in the bad states.

Written as a vector, w = (wG, wI, wS, wP) over Gain, Income, Stabilization and Protection, with cash the residual. When the regime layer of Chapter 11 is switched off, this vector collapses to a single scalar risk tilt θ between a risky block and a safe block, which is the lean default used through most of the book. When the layer is on, the four roles rotate as the cycle turns. Throughout, θ denotes the risk tilt and nothing else.

Admissibility

The time budget is the hard constraint. The shares are non-negative and sum to at most one, with the slack counted as leisure; the risk tilt lives in the unit interval and the role weights lie on the simplex.

τi≥0,∑iτi≤1,τL=1−∑iτi,θ∈[0,1](4.2)

The money side is constrained by liquidity: spending plus debt service cannot exceed income, asset yield and accessible liquid wealth, and a buffer floor keeps liquid wealth from going negative.

C+debt service≤Y+yields+accessible WL,WL≥Wbuf(4.3)

Two controls are discrete and fire only at events rather than every period: taking on leverage to acquire property (which converts liquid wealth into real assets and debt) and founding a company (which draws down a buffer and shifts the income process). These are handled as jumps in the state, described where the relevant goals are defined.

Assumption 4.1 — Separable effort

Time and money contribute additively to each capital’s growth, up to the saturation term. This is a simplification — in reality money can buy time and vice versa — but it keeps the control problem tractable and the recommendations interpretable. Chapter 20 sketches how to relax it.

Chapter 5

Dynamics

The equations of motion. Each is a stochastic differential equation; together they say how today’s state and controls, plus chance, produce tomorrow’s state.

Income

Income is the hinge between the capitals and wealth. It is Cobb–Douglas in expertise and network, scaled by the share of time spent earning and by health.

Y=AYτYφ(H)EaNb,a,b<1(5.1)
φ(H)=Hc(5.2)

The exponents a, b (both below one) encode diminishing returns and the fact that expertise and network are complements: doubling one does not double income, but neglecting either caps it. Health enters multiplicatively through φ, so a person running at reduced health earns less from the same hour.

Wealth accumulates

Define the net cash flowing into liquid wealth each instant,

F=Y+yields−C−mE−mN−pA−debt service(5.3)

then liquid wealth follows a controlled process whose drift and diffusion carry the role weights and, under the regime layer, the phase-dependent role returns.

where μm and Σm are the role-return mean and covariance in regime m. With the regime layer off they are constant and the vector collapses to the scalar tilt, recovering the familiar risky/safe blend.

μP=rf+θ(μM−rf),σP=θσM(5.5)

Real assets appreciate and yield, with their own shock; debt is drawn down by amortisation and jumps up when leverage is taken on.

Expertise, network and health saturate

Each of the three remaining capitals follows a logistic law: a growth term driven by time and money and by self-reinforcement, throttled by a saturation factor, minus depreciation, plus noise.

Ė=[gE(τE,mE)(1−E/E¯)−δE]E(5.8)
Ṅ=[gN(τN,mN)(1−N/N¯)−δN]N(5.9)
Ḣ=[gH(τH)(1−H)−δH(τY)]H(5.10)

The depreciation terms δ are the reason maintenance matters: stop investing and the capital drifts down. Health carries the model’s most important feedback. Its decay rate rises with overwork — time spent earning beyond a threshold accelerates the loss of the very health that makes earning productive.

δH(τY)=δH0+η·max(0,τY−τY∗)(5.11)

The habit adjusts toward recent consumption

The standard-of-living habit relaxes toward the level of consumption actually sustained, at a speed set by α. A step up in spending pulls the habit up over time; a cut leaves consumption below the accustomed level and is felt as a loss, which is the point of carrying it.

dκ=α(C−κ)dt(5.12)

Under the regime layer, the macro state is a continuous-time Markov chain with generator Q, so that over a small step the phase switches with probability set by the generator.

Pr[mt+dt=n∣mt=m]=Qmndt(5.13)
Assumption 5.1 — Mixed growth

Wealth obeys an accumulation law with no upper bound; expertise, network and health obey logistic laws with capital-specific ceilings; the habit obeys a mean-reverting law toward consumption. No single capital can grow without limit except wealth, and wealth cannot grow at all without the others feeding income.

Chapter 6

Coupling

The capitals are wired together. The single most consequential wire is the one that lets expertise, wealth and a maintained standard of living raise the ceiling on your network.

N¯=N0+λEE+λWW+λκκ(6.1)

Equation (6.1) says the network you can sustain is not fixed: competence (λE), resources (λW) and a maintained standard of living (λκ) open doors that would otherwise stay shut. The habit term is the new wire, and it is why a cut in spending is not free even when the goal is purely financial: it lowers the ceiling on the very network that lifts income. The coefficient λκ is the least identifiable in the model and is held small and stress-tested rather than estimated precisely (Chapter 11). A weak habit term may likewise be added to the expertise ceiling. The diagram gathers all the couplings in one place.

Solid = drives · dashed teal = raises a ceiling · dashed ochre = habit lifts the network ceiling / overwork erodes health · dotted = capitals tested against goals.

Two feedback loops deserve names. The reinforcing loop runs expertise → income → wealth → network ceiling → income: success compounds, and the habit reinforces it, since a maintained standard of living holds the ceiling up. The balancing loop runs earning → overwork → lower health → lower income: the system resists being pushed too hard. A healthy plan rides the first loop without tripping the second, and much of what the optimiser does is find that line.

Chapter 7

Goals as liabilities

Four archetypes cover most of a life. Each is a region of the state space, with a deadline and a required confidence.

Lump claim: a home

A purchase at price P demands two things at once — a deposit and the income to service the loan at a stressed rate.

WL≥ξPandaY≥icalc(1−ξ)P+μmaintP(7.1)

On execution the state jumps: liquid wealth falls by the deposit, real assets rise by the price, debt rises by the balance. The serviceability half of (7.1) is what a deposit-only rule of thumb misses, and it is what binds for young buyers.

Joint region: a company

Founding requires a cash buffer and a network and the expertise, simultaneously above threshold. No substitution is allowed — the intersection, not the sum.

WL≥Bbuf∧N≥Nmin∧E≥Emin(7.2)

Liability stream: retirement

Retirement is not a date but a stream. It is funded when assets plus the present value of future income cover the present value of future consumption and fixed claims.

Φ(t)=WL+hRWR−D+hHHCL(t)≥1(7.3)
L(t)=Et[∫tTc∗(s)M(t,s)ds+∑kGkM(t,Tk)](7.4)

Counterfactual: financial independence

Independence is the subtlest, and the most misunderstood. It is the point at which work becomes optional — whether or not the person stops. The test is a counterfactual: switch off labour income and ask whether passive cash flow plus a safe drawdown still covers desired spending. Two things matter. First, only drawable wealth counts — a residence you live in enters at a haircut hhome, which is zero, and income-producing real assets at a haircut hR.

Ωdraw=WL+hRWR−D,hhome=0(7.5)

Second, the condition can be written as a flow (passive income covers spend) or, equivalently, as a stock (drawable wealth plus the present value of non-labour income covers the present value of the spending stream, with labour income set to zero). Human capital is excluded from both, since the whole meaning of independence is that one need no longer work.

y(WL+hRWR−D)+yRWR≥c∗(7.6)
Ωdraw+PVnon-labour≥PVc∗(7.7)

Rearranging the flow condition gives the drawable wealth a person must hold at the target date. With a conservative sustainable real yield y and required real consumption c∗, net of the residence’s own operating yield, the perpetuity form is simply

Fundable∗=c∗y(7.8)
Definition 7.1 — Independence ≠ net worth

A goal defined on net worth Ω can be satisfied while the independence goal on drawable wealth Ωdraw is not. When wealth is concentrated in an illiquid residence, the two diverge sharply — the central fact of the independence and entrepreneur cases in Part III. Neither human capital nor the self-occupied residence enters the fundable base.

Chapter 8

Feasibility and uncertainty

The future is not a line. Feasibility is therefore not a yes or no but a probability, and the planner chooses how much of it to demand.

For each goal k we require that the probability of the trajectory entering the goal region by the deadline clears one minus a tolerance.

Pr(xTk∈Gk)≥1−εk(8.1)

This probability has no closed form for a system as coupled as ours, so we estimate it by Monte Carlo: simulate many futures under the candidate plan and count the fraction that succeed. The estimator and its standard error are elementary, which is a virtue — the reported confidence comes with its own error bar.

p̂=1M∑j=1M1{x(j)∈G}(8.2)

Constraining a probability directly is awkward for an optimiser: the count of successful scenarios is discontinuous in the controls. The standard remedy is to constrain the Conditional Value-at-Risk of the shortfall instead. CVaR is the expected loss in the worst ε tail, and it has a tractable variational form (Rockafellar–Uryasev).

CVaRε(L)=minζ{ζ+1εE[(L−ζ)+]}(8.3)

Requiring the CVaR of the negative goal-slack to be non-positive is a convex, conservative surrogate for the chance constraint (8.1): it implies feasibility at the required confidence, and penalises deep failures rather than merely counting them, one constraint per active goal.

CVaRεk(shortfallk)≤0(8.4)
Assumption 8.1 — Estimable dynamics

The model of the world is good enough that Monte-Carlo frequencies approximate true probabilities. The resulting numbers are well-reasoned estimates conditional on the model, not guarantees; Chapter 19 returns to this honestly.

Chapter 9

The objective

What are we optimising? A life lived well now, subject to keeping every goal on track with the confidence demanded of it.

Consumption enters through a constant-relative-risk-aversion utility, but of consumption measured against the habit: what is enjoyed is the margin of spending over the accustomed standard of living, which is how people actually experience a setback.

U(z)=z1−γ1−γ,z=C−κ(9.1)

The planner maximises expected discounted utility of habit-adjusted consumption and of leisure over the horizon, plus a terminal term for bequest or legacy.

maxu(·)E[∫0Te−ρt(U(C−κ)+ψv(τL,H))dt+e−ρTB(xT)](9.2)

The two terms are distinct channels and are not collapsed into one. Habit-adjusted consumption U(C−κ) runs through the reference stock of (5.12) and through the ceilings of (6.1); the leisure term ψ v(τL, H) runs through time to health and wellbeing, and is what stops the model prescribing a life of pure accumulation. Read plainly, the objective is: live as well as you can now, while keeping each goal funded to the confidence you require. Assembled with the constraints of Chapters 4–8, the full problem is the maximisation of (9.2) subject to the dynamics (5.1)–(5.13), the admissibility conditions (4.2)–(4.3), and one CVaR surrogate (8.4) per active goal, written in sample-average form for the solver of the next chapter.

Chapter 10

Solving it

A consumer cannot be handed a forty-year plan. The engine solves a finite-horizon problem from the current state, applies only this period’s action, and re-solves next period.

The optimum: a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

The optimal value function V(x, t) satisfies a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, carrying a regime-jump term when the layer is on. We state it once for rigour and then decline to grid it, because a state of this dimension makes a direct solution infeasible.

ρV=maxu∈U{U(C−κ)+ψv+Vt+∇V⊤f+12tr(σσ⊤∇xx2V)+∑nQmn(V(x,n)−V(x,m))}(10.1)

Discretise, then sample

The continuous dynamics are stepped with an Euler–Maruyama scheme, and the expectation in the objective is replaced by an average over M sampled scenarios — sample-average approximation. The first-period control is shared across all scenarios (non-anticipativity); later controls may adapt. This turns the stochastic program into a finite nonlinear program solvable at interactive speed.

xk+1=xk+f(xk,uk)Δt+σΔtζk(10.2)

Shadow prices: what an hour is worth

The Lagrangian of the discretised program attaches a multiplier to each state-transition constraint, and these multipliers are the costates — the marginal value of each capital at the optimum, read as the sensitivity of the optimal value to the initial state.

λ=∂J∗∂x0(10.3)

Their ratios are the exchange rates the model is really for. Comparing the value of an hour of networking against an hour of earning is a matter of comparing costate-weighted marginal effects,

ER=λN·∂Ṅ/∂τNλWL·∂Y/∂τY(10.4)

and whichever side is larger is where the next hour should go, for that goal, at that moment. The ranking is not a rule of thumb; it is computed from the person’s state and shifts as the state shifts. It is also the answer to the question people actually ask — not “will I make it?” but “what should I do tonight?”

Chapter 11

The regime layer and calibration

Two loose ends from Part II: the optional regime layer that couples a life to the capital cycle, and where the parameters come from and how to know whether they are right.

The regime layer

The regime m is the wire that ties the personal plan to the wider field model. Switched on, the role returns μm, Σm become phase-dependent, so the portfolio rotates across the four roles as the capital cycle turns: protection and stabilization carry weight into contraction and crisis, gain and income into expansion and boom, exactly as the institutional allocation does. The same cycle the macro notes describe therefore drives the returns the personal plan faces, and the personal and institutional problems become two instances of one machinery. Switched off, the model is the honest lean default of the earlier chapters, with constant returns and the scalar risk tilt θ. The layer is not a future extension but an option the adviser turns on when the cycle view is part of the mandate.

Validation 11.1 — the Merton limit

With a single liquid capital, no goals, no habit and constant returns, the problem must collapse to Merton’s rule, the optimal risky weight w∗ = (μ − r)/(γσ2) with consumption a fixed fraction of wealth. Reproducing this is a unit test the engine must pass, not the framing of the model.

Where the parameters come from

The parameters fall into three groups. Market parameters (role returns, volatilities, rates) come from standard financial data and are common across users. Structural parameters (the growth, saturation and depreciation rates of the capitals; the income exponents; the habit speed α) are estimated from panel data on careers, earnings and health, and are broadly shared. Personal parameters (initial state, risk aversion, the leisure weight, goal thresholds) are elicited from the individual. Only the last group must be set per person; the first two are the product’s calibrated backbone.

The honest test of a structural calibration is whether it reproduces real trajectories. A person whose past is known becomes a backtest: run the dynamics forward from their early state with their actual choices, and check that the model recovers their observed capitals and wealth at known dates. A companion case study does exactly this over a twenty-six-year record; if the fitted dynamics cannot retrodict a life that was actually lived, they have no business predicting one that has not been.

Assumption 11.2 — Identifiability

Not every parameter is separately identifiable from a single life. Where data cannot pin a parameter down (the habit-to-network coupling λκ is the clearest case), it is fixed small from population priors and its influence is stress-tested by sensitivity analysis rather than estimated spuriously.

The default parameter values used throughout the worked lives are collected in Appendix B. They are chosen to make the model’s behaviour legible and internally consistent, not fitted to any individual; a real deployment would replace them with calibrated values and report the sensitivity of each recommendation to the least certain among them.

Chapter 12

How to read a case

The framework is a generic stochastic control problem, not a personal model. Its content is what happens when it is instantiated with a specific capital mix, goal set, risk aversion and constraints. The next five chapters are that test.

Everything that distinguishes one life from another enters as a parameter of the same control problem: the initial capitals (including the starting habit κ0), the dated goals with their required confidences εk, the risk aversion γ, and the constraints on labour, liquidity and permitted assets. A case is a choice of that tuple; the solver returns a state-contingent policy for consumption, the time budget, capital investment, the role weights and the discrete jumps. The five lives differ only in this tuple.

(K0,{(Gk,Tk,εk)},c∗,γ,{constraints})(12.1)

Each chapter opens with the person and their goal as a formal liability, gives the initial state (the six capitals as numbers, with the starting habit noted), states the parameters that differ from the Appendix B defaults, shows what the engine outputs, and identifies the binding constraint. It closes with a lever. Wealth figures are in Swiss francs; the indices E, N, H are on the normalised zero-to-one scale of Chapter 3. Numbers are illustrative — the point is the shape of the reasoning, not the digits. A sixth life, a real twenty-six-year trajectory used to calibrate the dynamics, is treated separately as a companion case study.

The human-capital-adjusted Merton rule. With human capital HC several times financial wealth W, and a high effective beta βHC of that human capital to the Gain role, the optimal Gain weight scales to total wealth and is then offset by the market exposure the career already supplies.

wG∗=μ−rγσ2·W+HCW−βHCHCW(13.1)

With HC large and βHC high, the second term dominates and wG∗ is driven low or even negative: the young professional should hold a financial book tilted to Income and Protection, not chase Gain.

Output and binding constraint. The policy also directs heavy investment of time and money into expertise and network, because their S-curves are still steep and their marginal return exceeds that of financial assets. The binding lever is not the portfolio but the allocation of hours: the exchange rate of Chapter 10 favours building and hedging the human capitals over trading the financial ones.

Lever — hold the financial book defensive (Income and Protection, cash the residual) and pour the marginal hour into expertise and network while their S-curves are steep. The plan for the young is to build the human capitals and hedge them, not to double a career bet in the portfolio.

The tail shock
a scenario, not a person · job loss, disability, a health event
Chapter 17
Archetype:
tail constraint

The last case is not a different person but a different scenario for any of them: the realised tail. The tail constraint exists precisely to pre-position for it, so the plan carries liquidity and insurance that look excessive in the central case and prove exactly sufficient in the bad one.

Goal. Hold, before any shock, a buffer B in the Protection role and a hedge q large enough that the funding shortfall in the worst ε tail stays within tolerance, i.e. so that (8.4) holds when a shock impairs human capital and raises liabilities at once.

Sizing. The requirement follows directly: the buffer plus hedge payout must cover the drop in fundable capital plus the rise in liabilities across the tail scenarios, evaluated at the ε quantile.

B+hedge payout≥(ΔL−Δfundable)tail(17.1)

Output and binding constraint. Requiring CVaRε(shortfall) ≤ 0 forces the plan to provision in advance rather than after the event, when provisioning is impossible or ruinous. The binding constraint is the tail itself: the worst ε of scenarios sets the buffer, and the central case merely carries it. This is the entire purpose of solving with a tail constraint rather than to an expectation.

Lever — size the Protection buffer and any hedge to the tail, not to the average; the provision that looks like drag in the central case is exactly what keeps the plan solvent when the shock lands.
Chapter 18

What the five have in common

Across the five, the four-capital state, the two budgets and the role-based portfolio, the habit-adjusted objective, the per-goal tail constraints, and the model-predictive solver never change. Only the parameter tuple (12.1) does.

That the same machinery produces a defensive book for one, a four-million drawable target for another, a preservation glide for a third, forced diversification for a fourth, and a pre-positioned protection buffer for the fifth is the demonstration that the framework is generic and the cases are instantiations, not a personal recipe dressed as a method.

Three lessons recur. First, the binding constraint is usually not money. For the young professional it is the market beta of a career; for the independence-seeker, drawable liquidity against a walled-off residence; for the near-retiree, sequence risk in the tail; for the entrepreneur, concentration; for the tail case, the worst-ε scenario itself. A money-only tool would have missed the real lever in every case.

Second, time and health are the quiet limiters. They do not appear on a bank statement, yet they cap what every other capital can produce, and the overwork loop means they can be spent without noticing until the bill arrives. The model keeps them on the balance sheet precisely so they cannot be quietly overdrawn.

Third, the tail is where a plan earns its keep. A conventional glide path, a fixed savings rate and a single risk questionnaire hand materially different lives the same template and are wrong for almost all of them. The framework beats the cookie-cutter by solving each life’s actual problem with its actual capitals, goals and constraints, and by re-solving as the state evolves. Together with the balance-sheet and control notes, this closes the loop with the institutional Optimizer: the same coherent, tail-aware, role-based, regime-conditional machinery governs a pension fund and a life, because both are capitals funding liabilities across an uncertain cycle.

Chapter 19

Honest limits

A model is a lens, not a crystal ball. Being clear about its limits is what makes it safe to use.

The model reduces a person to a handful of capitals and a habit. Real lives are richer, and the things hardest to quantify — judgement, love, luck, meaning — often matter most. The capitals are chosen because they are the ones a plan can act on, not because they are all that count.

The parameters are illustrative unless calibrated, and the probabilities are conditional on the model being right about how the world works, which it never fully is. A reported “85% chance” is a well-reasoned estimate inside a particular set of assumptions, not a law of nature. The right use is comparative — this plan versus that one — rather than absolute. The habit-to-network coupling λκ is the least identifiable parameter and is deliberately held small; a recommendation that hinges on it should be treated with suspicion.

The optimiser can also be confidently wrong in a specific way: if a capital or coupling is mis-specified, the shadow prices will point somewhere plausible and mistaken. This is why the calibration backtest and the sensitivity analysis are not optional extras but part of the method.

Finally, nothing here is personalised financial, tax, or legal advice. It is a way of thinking and a tool for exploring trade-offs. What it offers over a money-only plan is an honest accounting: time and health on the balance sheet, goals as claims that can be missed, a standard of living that compounds, and the trade-offs between them priced in a common currency. Used to inform judgement rather than replace it, it earns its keep; used as an oracle, it will mislead like any other.

Chapter 20

Extensions

Directions in which the model naturally grows, each already anticipated by the structure.

1   Expertise as a vector

Chapter 3 treats expertise as a scalar. Serial reinvention — the move from one field to another — is better modelled by promoting E to a vector of domain-specific capitals, where a pivot seeds a new component and the existing network accelerates its ceiling. The scalar version is the special case of a single active domain.

2   Households and dependants

A single-person state can be extended by coupling two state vectors through shared wealth, correlated time budgets, and children as a time-and-money liability with its own schedule.

3   Taxes and institutions

Pension pillars, mortgage rules, and tax treatment are jurisdiction-specific and enter as constraints and cash-flow adjustments. They do not change the structure but they change the numbers, sometimes decisively; the reference implementation already carries a Swiss-style progressive income tax and a wealth tax.

4   The regime layer, already switched on

The regime-switching dynamics that once sat here as a future extension are now part of the model proper (Chapters 3, 5 and 11): the constant parameters can be made regime-dependent so returns, volatilities and even growth rates shift with a slow-moving macro state. What remains as extension is richer regime estimation and a fuller two-way coupling with the institutional cycle model.

5   Behaviour and adherence

The largest gap between a plan and a life is adherence. Modelling the person’s own follow-through — the probability that a recommended action is actually taken — would let the engine prefer plans that are not merely optimal but likely to be followed.

None of these require abandoning the framework; each is a place where more structure can be added when the data and the use case justify it. That is the mark of a model built to grow rather than to impress.

Appendix C

The engine, in pseudocode

The receding-horizon loop of Chapter 10, as it would be implemented.

initialise x ← observed state (onboarding), regime m if the layer is on for each period k = 0, 1, 2, ...: active ← goals not yet met and not past deadline sample M scenarios of the shocks (and regime path) over horizon H solve the finite-horizon NLP (SAA objective with habit-adjusted U(C-κ); CVaR surrogate per active goal; time + money constraints; role-weight simplex or scalar tilt) → optimal control path u*, costates λ apply only u*[0] # this period's action read shadow prices from λ # the exchange rates x ← observe new state # real data, or simulate one step emit: P(goal) per goal, funding ratio, u*[0] in words, top exchange-rate insight, any early warning

The inner NLP is solved with an interior-point method; automatic differentiation supplies both the gradients and, through the constraint multipliers, the costates that become the exchange rates. Typical settings: monthly step, a horizon running to the last goal’s deadline, and a few hundred scenarios — enough for stable probabilities at interactive speed.
