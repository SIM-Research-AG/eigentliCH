Preface

Why a balance sheet for a life

This book takes an idea from institutional finance — asset–liability management — and asks what happens if we apply it honestly to a single human life.

A pension fund does not measure success by how much cash it holds. It asks whether its assets will meet its liabilities as they fall due, under uncertainty, with an acceptable margin of safety. That question — not “how much do we have?” but “will what we have meet what we owe, on time, probably?” — turns out to be exactly the right one to ask of a person deciding how to spend the next decade.

The trouble with money-only planning is not that money is unimportant; it is that money is downstream. What actually produces the money, and the life, is a set of slower-moving capitals: what you know, who you know, and the health and hours you have to deploy them. Those capitals grow in a characteristic way, they feed one another, and they decay if neglected. A model that keeps them on the balance sheet can say things a cash-flow spreadsheet cannot — most usefully, what an hour of your time is actually worth, and toward which goal.

The book has four parts. Part I motivates the reframe and the dynamical-systems view. Part II builds the model in full: the state, the controls, the stochastic dynamics, the goals, the notion of feasibility, and the optimisation that turns all of it into a recommendation. This part is deliberately mathematical; readers who want the argument without the machinery can follow the prose and skip the displayed equations without losing the thread. Part III works five lives in detail, each chosen to stress a different part of the model. Part IV is candid about what the model cannot do, and where it should go next. Everything numerical here is illustrative, chosen to make the behaviour legible; none of it is financial advice.

Chapter 1

The reframe

Three moves turn a life into something an asset–liability manager would recognise. Each is small on its own; together they change what questions we can ask.

Move one: capitals are assets

We treat everything that carries value across time as an asset. Financial wealth is the obvious one, but it is joined by expertise (what you can do), network (who will act on your behalf), and health (the energy that makes the other three usable). These are not metaphors. Each can be invested in, each yields a return, and each depreciates if left alone. The difference from money is only that we lack a tidy account statement for them — which is precisely the gap the model fills.

Move two: goals are liabilities

A milestone — a home, a company, financial independence, retirement — is a claim: an amount, due at a date, that the assets must be able to meet. Framing goals this way imports the whole apparatus of liability management: matching, funding ratios, duration, and the discipline of asking not whether a goal is nice to have but whether it is funded.

Definition 1.1 — Liability

A goal is a triple (deadline, region, confidence): a time by which it must be met, a set of states that count as meeting it, and the probability of success the planner requires. A goal with no required confidence is a wish, not a liability.

Move three: the saving rate is an output, and time is the constraint

The two moves above are natural; the third is where most planning goes wrong. People treat the saving rate as a dial they set. It is not. The saving rate is what remains after consumption and self-investment — an output of the plan, not an input to it. Set the inputs and the saving rate is determined; try to set the saving rate directly and you have merely hidden the real decisions.

And the truly scarce resource is not money but time. Money can be borrowed, saved, and compounded; the twenty-four-hour day cannot. Every hour spent earning is an hour not spent learning, connecting, or recovering. A plan that does not treat the time budget as a hard constraint will always look more feasible on paper than it is in a life — because on paper, time is free.

Assumption 1.2 — Time is binding

The allocation of time is constrained to a fixed budget each period and cannot be stored or transferred across periods. This single assumption is responsible for most of the model’s more useful and less obvious recommendations.

The reward for the extra structure is a richer question. A money-only tool asks “will I have enough?” This model asks “what should I do this period, given everything I am trying to become, and what is the marginal hour worth toward each goal?” The rest of the book is the machinery that answers it.

Chapter 2

A person as a dynamical system

If capitals are assets and goals are liabilities, then a life is a controlled dynamical system: a state that evolves over time, steered by decisions, disturbed by chance, aiming to reach target regions.

Control theory gives us the vocabulary. A state summarises everything about the present that matters for the future. A control is what the decision-maker can set. Dynamics describe how the state responds to the control and to random disturbance. A goal is a region of the state space we would like to enter; feasibility is the question of whether the reachable set of the controlled system intersects that region before the deadline. This is the same structure an engineer uses to regulate a plant — applied, here, to a career and a portfolio.

The characteristic motion of each capital is the S-curve. Growth is slow while a capital is small, accelerates once it is large enough to reinforce itself (skill compounds; networks introduce you to more networks), and flattens as it approaches a natural ceiling. This is the logistic law, and it is why timing matters so much: the same effort produces almost nothing in the slow phase and a great deal in the accelerating one.

Goals-as-regions is not a formality. Because the capitals are distinct dimensions, a goal can require several of them at once — and no amount of one can substitute for a shortfall in another. Founding a company is the clean example: money without a network, or a network without the competence to use it, does not clear the bar. The phase portrait below shows why the path you take through the state space, not just the effort you expend, decides whether you arrive.

With the picture in place, Part II makes each element precise: what exactly is in the state, what the controls are and how they are constrained, the equations of motion, the shape of the goals, and the optimisation that chooses the controls.

The remaining three capitals are dimensionless indices, normalised so that a well-developed professional sits near 1.0: E for expertise, N for network, and H for health, bounded in [0,1] and acting as an efficiency multiplier on everything the person does. Health earns its place in the state precisely because it closes a feedback loop a static plan would miss: overwork erodes it, and its erosion quietly lowers the return on all the other capitals.

Definition 3.1 — Capital

A capital is a stock that (i) carries value across periods, (ii) can be increased by allocating time or money, (iii) yields a flow return, and (iv) depreciates without maintenance. Wealth satisfies (i)–(iii) and, uniquely, does not depreciate — it earns a return instead.

Two properties distinguish the capitals mathematically, and Chapter 5 respects both. Expertise, network and health saturate — each rises toward a ceiling. Wealth accumulates — it compounds without one. Modelling all four with a single tidy law would be elegant and wrong.

Chapter 4

The controls: two budgets

You cannot move the capitals directly. You move them by allocating time and money each period. The control vector collects every lever the planner actually holds.

(4.1)

The time shares τ divide the productive day between earning, learning, networking and recovery. The money flows are consumption C, education and network spend mE, mN, and amortisation pA. Admissibility

The time budget is the hard constraint. The shares are non-negative and sum to at most one, with the slack counted as leisure; the risk tilt lives in the unit interval.

(4.2)

The money side is constrained by liquidity: spending plus debt service cannot exceed income, asset yield and accessible liquid wealth, and a buffer floor keeps liquid wealth from going negative.

(4.3)

Two controls are discrete and fire only at events rather than every period: taking on leverage to acquire property (which converts liquid wealth into real assets and debt) and founding a company (which draws down a buffer and shifts the income process). These are handled as jumps in the state, described where the relevant goals are defined.

Assumption 4.1 — Separable effort

Time and money contribute additively to each capital’s growth, up to the saturation term. This is a simplification — in reality money can buy time and vice versa — but it keeps the control problem tractable and the recommendations interpretable. Chapter 20 sketches how to relax it.

Two feedback loops deserve names. The reinforcing loop runs expertise → income → wealth → network ceiling → income: success compounds. The balancing loop runs earning → overwork → lower health → lower income: the system resists being pushed too hard. A healthy plan rides the first loop without tripping the second, and much of what the optimiser does is find that line.

Chapter 8

Feasibility and uncertainty

The future is not a line. Feasibility is therefore not a yes or no but a probability, and the planner chooses how much of it to demand.

For each goal k we require that the probability of the trajectory entering the goal region by the deadline clears one minus a tolerance.

(8.1)

This probability has no closed form for a system as coupled as ours, so we estimate it by Monte Carlo: simulate many futures under the candidate plan and count the fraction that succeed. The estimator and its standard error are elementary, which is a virtue — the reported confidence comes with its own error bar.

(8.2)

Constraining a probability directly is awkward for an optimiser: the count of successful scenarios is discontinuous in the controls. The standard remedy is to constrain the Conditional Value-at-Risk of the shortfall instead. CVaR is the expected loss in the worst ε tail, and it has a tractable variational form.

(8.3)

Requiring the CVaR of the negative goal-slack to be non-positive is a convex, conservative surrogate for the chance constraint (8.1): it implies feasibility at the required confidence, and penalises deep failures rather than merely counting them.

(8.4)
Assumption 8.1 — Estimable dynamics

The model of the world is good enough that Monte-Carlo frequencies approximate true probabilities. The resulting numbers are well-reasoned estimates conditional on the model, not guarantees; Chapter 19 returns to this honestly.

Chapter 10

Solving it

A consumer cannot be handed a forty-year plan. The engine solves a finite-horizon problem from the current state, applies only this period’s action, and re-solves next period.

Discretise, then sample

The continuous dynamics are stepped with an Euler–Maruyama scheme,

(10.1)

and the expectation in the objective is replaced by an average over M sampled scenarios — sample-average approximation. The first-period control is shared across all scenarios (non-anticipativity); later controls may adapt. This turns the stochastic program into a finite nonlinear program solvable at interactive speed.

It is also the answer to the question people actually ask — not “will I make it?” but “what should I do tonight?”

Chapter 11

Calibration

A model is only as good as its parameters. This chapter says where they come from and how to know whether they are right.

The parameters fall into three groups. Market parameters (returns, volatilities, rates) come from standard financial data and are common across users. Structural parameters (the growth, saturation and depreciation rates of the capitals; the income exponents) are estimated from panel data on careers, earnings and health, and are broadly shared. Personal parameters (initial state, risk aversion, the leisure weight, goal thresholds) are elicited from the individual. Only the last group must be set per person; the first two are the product’s calibrated backbone.

The honest test of a structural calibration is whether it reproduces real trajectories. A person whose past is known becomes a backtest: run the dynamics forward from their early state with their actual choices, and check that the model recovers their observed capitals and wealth at known dates. Chapter 16 does exactly this over a twenty-six-year record. If the fitted dynamics cannot retrodict a life that was actually lived, they have no business predicting one that has not been.

Assumption 11.1 — Identifiability

Not every parameter is separately identifiable from a single life. Where data cannot pin a parameter down, it is fixed from population priors and its influence is stress-tested by sensitivity analysis rather than estimated spuriously.

The default parameter values used throughout the case studies are collected in Appendix B. They are chosen to make the model’s behaviour legible and internally consistent, not fitted to any individual; a real deployment would replace them with calibrated values and report the sensitivity of each recommendation to the least certain among them.

Every case opens with the person, their goal as a formal liability, and their initial state — the six capitals as numbers. The backtest: nineteen to forty-five

A life that was actually lived is the sternest test of the dynamics. From age nineteen — the hardest available programme, control-systems theory at ETH, funded by working to sixty per cent while building a trading system — the capitals should trace a particular shape if the model is right. The trading system converts early expertise into liquid wealth, which at twenty-six is enough to acquire a property. Founding a research firm at twenty-nine while keeping a part-time teaching role is, in the model’s language, a deliberate sacrifice of some earning time to raise the network ceiling through equation (6.1); the payoff arrives years later as board seats and mandates that would have been unreachable earlier. The fit is not merely decorative. It disciplines the structural parameters: the income exponents, the network-ceiling couplings, and the property dynamics are all constrained by having to reproduce this record. A calibration that could not retrodict the trading-funded purchase or the post-founding network acceleration would be rejected.

Chapter 18

What the five have in common

Read side by side, the five cases tell one story: money is rarely the binding constraint, and the profile of capitals matters more than their total.

Three lessons recur. First, the binding constraint is usually not money. For Maya it was future earning power; for Jonas and Lena, spending discipline; for Amara, network; for Nicolas, drawable liquidity and time; for David, the sequencing of a glide. A money-only tool would have missed the real lever in every case.

Second, time and health are the quiet limiters. They do not appear on a bank statement, yet they cap what every other capital can produce, and the overwork loop means they can be spent without noticing until the bill arrives. The model keeps them on the balance sheet precisely so they cannot be quietly overdrawn.

Third, timing beats effort. Because capitals grow on S-curves and ceilings can be raised, the same investment pays very differently depending on when it is made and in which order. Building expertise early to lift a network ceiling later, or converting a relationship into a client before founding, are not clever tricks — they are what the structure of the model rewards.

Chapter 19

Honest limits

A model is a lens, not a crystal ball. Being clear about its limits is what makes it safe to use.

The model reduces a person to a handful of capitals. Real lives are richer, and the things hardest to quantify — judgement, love, luck, meaning — often matter most. The four capitals are chosen because they are the ones a plan can act on, not because they are all that count.

The parameters are illustrative unless calibrated, and the probabilities are conditional on the model being right about how the world works, which it never fully is. A reported “82% chance” is a well-reasoned estimate inside a particular set of assumptions, not a law of nature. The right use is comparative — this plan versus that one — rather than absolute.

The optimiser can also be confidently wrong in a specific way: if a capital or coupling is mis-specified, the shadow prices will point somewhere plausible and mistaken. This is why the calibration backtest and the sensitivity analysis are not optional extras but part of the method.

Finally, nothing here is personalised financial, tax, or legal advice. It is a way of thinking and a tool for exploring trade-offs. What it offers over a money-only plan is an honest accounting: time and health on the balance sheet, goals as claims that can be missed, and the trade-offs between them priced in a common currency. Used to inform judgement rather than replace it, it earns its keep; used as an oracle, it will mislead like any other.

Chapter 20

Extensions

Five directions in which the model naturally grows, each already anticipated by the structure.

1   Expertise as a vector

Chapter 3 treats expertise as a scalar. Serial reinvention — the move from one field to another that Chapter 16’s subject exemplifies — is better modelled by promoting E to a vector of domain-specific capitals, where a pivot seeds a new component and the existing network accelerates its ceiling. The scalar version is the special case of a single active domain.

2   Households and dependants

Chapter 14 collapsed a couple into one state. A fuller treatment couples two state vectors through shared wealth, correlated time budgets, and children as a time-and-money liability with its own schedule.

3   Taxes and institutions

Pension pillars, mortgage rules, and tax treatment are jurisdiction-specific and enter as constraints and cash-flow adjustments. They do not change the structure but they change the numbers, sometimes decisively.

4   Regime-switching dynamics

The constant parameters of Part II can be made regime-dependent, so that returns, volatilities and even growth rates shift with a slow-moving macro state — a natural bridge to the author’s wider work on cycles.

5   Behaviour and adherence

The largest gap between a plan and a life is adherence. Modelling the person’s own follow-through — the probability that a recommended action is actually taken — would let the engine prefer plans that are not merely optimal but likely to be followed.

None of these require abandoning the framework; each is a place where more structure can be added when the data and the use case justify it. That is the mark of a model built to grow rather than to impress.

Appendix C

The engine, in pseudocode

The receding-horizon loop of Chapter 10, as it would be implemented.

initialise x ← observed state (onboarding) for each period k = 0, 1, 2, ...: active ← goals not yet met and not past deadline sample M scenarios of the shocks over the horizon H solve the finite-horizon NLP (SAA objective; CVaR surrogate per active goal; time + money constraints) → optimal control path u*, costates λ apply only u*[0] # this period's action read shadow prices from λ # the exchange rates x ← observe new state # real data, or simulate one step emit: P(goal) per goal, funding ratio, u*[0] in words, top exchange-rate insight, any early warning

The inner NLP is solved with an interior-point method; automatic differentiation supplies both the gradients and, through the constraint multipliers, the costates that become the exchange rates. Typical settings: monthly step, a horizon running to the last goal’s deadline, and a few hundred scenarios — enough for stable probabilities at interactive speed.
